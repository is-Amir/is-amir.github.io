# **1\. Proposed program of research and application to soft tissue surgery simulation**

My proposed research program would develop and evaluate real-time soft-tissue simulation for surgical training, initially focusing on head-and-neck oncology and reconstruction. The central question is which aspects of tissue behavior must be reproduced accurately to support deliberate practice and objective assessment of tissue-handling skills. My foundation is image-based modeling, nonlinear soft-tissue simulation, and systematic evaluation of reconstructive choices, demonstrated through hiFEM, VSP-BioSim, and benchtop experiments \[1–3\]. I would extend this experience toward interactive tissue handling and surgical education. This direction builds on the team’s published work: Lui et al. identified logistical and resource barriers to integrating simulation into training workflows \[4\]; CardinalSim participants requested soft-tissue representation and superior haptic feedback \[5\]; and the Calgary Simulation Curriculum identifies objective performance assessment as a next step \[6\].

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;I would investigate how anatomy, material properties, and modeling assumptions affect tissue response. With the biomechanics team, I would plan tissue tests and fit model parameters to the measurements. Sensitivity analysis would identify which properties most affect the predictions. Independent measurements would test the reference model, followed by comparisons to quantify the additional error introduced by faster computation. For real-time simulation, I would review complementary strategies: energy-based solvers for deformable mechanics and contact \[7–10\]; constraint-projection methods \[11–13\]; reduced-order models \[14\]; and learned deformation and force surrogates, including physics-informed hybrids \[15–21\]. I would also examine methods for cutting and suturing, including material point methods, fracture models based on position-based dynamics, and extended finite-element methods \[22–25\]. These approaches overlap and can be combined. Starting with the team’s available implementation, I would benchmark selected methods on the same task for deformation and force accuracy, response time, stability, and integration requirements. I would then develop or adapt the most promising approach and test its performance across relevant anatomical and material variations.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;An initial application could be controlled tissue retraction to expose a target during a neck-dissection exercise. I would agree on the task and learning objectives with SCULPT’s clinical team, develop the model, and work with the software team to integrate it into the simulation platform. Trainees could practice repeatedly and review how they moved the instrument, displaced the tissue, and maintained exposure. The simulator could display cumulative mechanical trauma, localized tissue strains, and handling stability metrics, once those estimates had been validated against physical measurements. Energy-based haptic rendering could also let trainees feel tissue resistance through the instrument \[26\]. Drawing on the team’s microlaryngoscopy OSATS study \[27\], I would work with surgical educators to develop task-specific assessment and guidance for improving performance. Subsequent studies would test whether more accurate simulation of tissue behavior, such as viscoelasticity, improves learning under otherwise comparable training conditions \[28\]. Assessment would distinguish performance during practice, retention after a delay, and transfer to a separate physical task.

# **2\. Expected program outcomes at years 1, 3, and 5**

**Year 1**: I expect to deliver a focused mechanical benchmark, a reference model with documented accuracy and limitations, and a comparison of selected interactive (real-time) methods for one agreed head-and-neck task. Outputs would include a tested software interface, a reproducible research package, a manuscript submitted for publication, and a pilot training and assessment protocol developed with the education team. I would also pursue eligible external awards and contribute to team grant applications. These deliverables ensure immediate, measurable return.

**Year 3**: I expect an integrated training module covering anatomical and material variations, with evidence showing which computational simplifications preserve relevant tissue responses. I would apply the imaging-to-model automation skills demonstrated in VSP-BioSim to build a large database of patient-specific anatomical models \[2\]. Evaluation would gather face, content, and construct validity evidence for the platform and its assessments, following the team’s educational research \[5,6,27\]. Learner studies would test whether selected mechanical features or performance guidance improve tissue-handling skills and retention. With continued support, I would publish these findings, release reusable models and assessment tools, and mentor students conducting modeling and validation projects.

**Year 5**: I aim to lead an externally supported research program spanning several head-and-neck training tasks, including reconstructive tissue handling. Collaborative studies would test skill transfer to physical tasks, and multicenter studies would assess educational effectiveness across training programs. Together, these studies would help determine whether and when additional mechanical detail improves learning and guide model selection for surgical curricula \[28\]. Once the training platform is established, I would adapt its models for interactive, patient-specific virtual surgical planning and validate their predictions against surgical outcomes \[1,2\].

# **3\. Philosophy and approach to mentorship and teaching**

I approach mentorship by helping people build the skills and confidence to take ownership of unfamiliar research problems. During my PhD, this meant guiding a student in building a database of tongue segmentations from MRI images using semi-automated tools. In another ongoing mentorship, I have supported an undergraduate researcher with a nursing background as his work has progressed from preparing silicone phantoms and setting up benchtop experiments to developing drafts of a scoping review and a feasibility study of breast-reconstruction planning with my hiFEM software. To support that progression, we use weekly meetings to discuss questions and review manageable milestones. I guide his critical reading and use worked examples to connect the computational methods to the clinical problem, adjusting support as he takes on greater responsibility. This approach builds on my earlier experience during my master’s research, when I helped a graduate student develop an image-processing method for experimental strain measurement. The same emphasis on independence guides my informal mentorship of health researchers: I help with statistical analysis and MATLAB/Python workflows, providing documentation so colleagues can understand and repeat the methods themselves. My own experience with a multidisciplinary doctoral committee and clinical collaborators has also shaped how I offer this support. Learning from their expertise taught me to connect technical explanations to clinical questions. I bring that same learning mindset into how I mentor others.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In teaching, I want students to explain their decisions and recognize when a result needs further scrutiny. As a teaching assistant in Strength of Materials and Computational Mechanics of Metal Forming, I delivered lectures, worked through examples, assigned projects, and supported students individually. I structured practical work so students could learn by identifying and correcting errors. For instance, I proposed simplifying the final complex modeling project so students could assess their work against an established reference solution. This gave them a concrete basis for investigating errors and understanding the importance of verification and validation appropriate to the model’s purpose. Sensitivity analysis extended that scrutiny to how their assumptions affected the conclusions. To address difficulties as they arose, I collected questions before weekly sessions and used them to guide group discussion and individual feedback. My teaching also includes an invited seminar on virtual surgical planning for international medical trainees at UoA. At SCULPT, I would draw on this experience to support learners across disciplines and work with clinical educators to design practice activities with clear objectives and feedback that helps learners improve.
